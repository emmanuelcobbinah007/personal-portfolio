In the past six months, I’ve built multiple products.  
Some survived.  
Most didn’t.

![](/notes/nothing-hurts-more-than-watching-your-dreams-die/image-1.jpeg)

Looking back, many of those deaths were avoidable.

But when you have what feels like a million-dollar idea, hype has a funny way of silencing reason.

Most first-time founders learn this lesson the hard way. I did.  
But the few who get it right on their first try tend to do something different before writing a single line of code.

So here are the things I wish I had known six months ago to give my ideas a fighting chance.

### 1. Don’t be stingy with ideas. They’re almost worthless.

A lot of founders treat ideas like treasure.

They hide them.  
They’re scared someone will “steal” them.

But ideas are cheap. Execution is what matters.

If you’re afraid to talk about your idea, you’ll never pressure-test it. And the market will be the one to reject it later.

### 2. Do the boring research.

Before building anything, talk to people.

Run surveys. Do interviews. Study competitors. Look at existing solutions. Identify your ICP (Ideal Customer Profile).

This part isn’t exciting, but if you skip it, you’ll end up with a brilliant solution in desperate need of a problem.

### 3. Think about how it will make money from the start.

You’ll often hear people say:

“Just solve the problem and the money will follow.”

And yes, there’s some truth to that.  
But masa… you’re still a human being. You have to eat.

Understanding how your idea makes money forces you to understand who values it enough to pay for it. Besides, it’s also good for investor pitches.

### 4. Study the system your solution will operate in.

Sometimes the idea itself is fine.

The environment is the problem.

Infrastructure, regulations, user habits, payment systems, culture, all of these shape whether a solution can survive.

A great idea in the wrong ecosystem can still fail.

### 5. Make sure it’s dead simple to explain.

If your idea takes five minutes and a whiteboard to explain, that’s a problem.

Your users should be able to describe it to someone else in one sentence.

Because your first customers are also your first marketers.  
If they can’t explain it, the idea doesn’t spread.

And if it doesn’t spread… it dies.

### 6. Competition is not the enemy.

More competition usually means more opportunity.

It means people are already spending money in that market.

The real danger isn’t competition.  
It’s building something nobody cares about.

### 7. Solve real problems.

This is obvious, but it’s easy to miss.

A real problem is something people are already trying to solve, even if the solutions are messy.

Those messy solutions are often the signal that an opportunity exists.

And only after all of this…  
Then you start building.

Because the goal of this phase isn’t to prove your idea is brilliant.  
It’s to prove it deserves to exist.
